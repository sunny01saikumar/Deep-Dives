A rain delay is a problem for limited-overs cricket. If the second team gets fewer overs than the first, simply scaling the target down is unfair, because the batting side with fewer overs also has fewer chances to score.

## Two resources, not one

The Duckworth-Lewis method, created by statisticians Frank Duckworth and Tony Lewis, treats a batting side as having two resources: **overs left** and **wickets in hand**. Having ten wickets and fifty overs is the most a team can have. Every over played and every wicket lost uses some of it up.

## How the target is reset

The method turns the resources each team used into percentages. If the second team will have access to fewer resources than the first, the target is scaled down in proportion. If it will have more, the target goes up. The method was later updated by Professor Steven Stern and is now called Duckworth-Lewis-Stern, or DLS.

## Why wickets matter

Two teams can both be given thirty overs and still have very different chances. A team batting with all ten wickets can attack from ball one. A team that had already lost five wickets before a rain break cannot. DLS tries to reflect that difference.

## What to look for in a match

When a break happens, officials announce a revised target and overs. If you want to check the fairness yourself, compare the resources: the side with more overs and more wickets in hand is the one expected to score more.
