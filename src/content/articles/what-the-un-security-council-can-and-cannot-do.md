The United Nations Security Council is the one UN body whose decisions are legally binding on member states. That power is why its votes matter, and why the way it votes is so often the story.

## Who sits on it

The council has fifteen members. Five are permanent: China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States. The other ten are elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms, with seats shared out across regions.

## How a decision is made

A substantive resolution needs at least nine votes in favour. It also needs every permanent member to avoid voting against it. A single "no" from a permanent member is a veto, and the resolution fails even if the other fourteen vote yes. An abstention does not block a resolution.

## What the council can do

- Call for ceasefires and ask parties to negotiate
- Impose sanctions, such as travel bans or arms embargoes
- Set up and extend peacekeeping missions
- Authorise the use of force under Chapter VII of the UN Charter

## What it cannot do alone

The council has no army of its own. Peacekeepers and any enforcement come from troops that member states agree to provide. It also cannot act when a permanent member blocks it, which is the main reason it can stall on the conflicts that most need a response.

## Why it matters to readers

When a headline says the council "failed to agree", it usually means one of two things: fewer than nine members voted yes, or a permanent member used its veto. The difference tells you whether the problem is a lack of support or a single objection.
